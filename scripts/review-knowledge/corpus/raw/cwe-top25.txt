<h2 class="header">CWE Top 25 Most Dangerous Software Weaknesses</h2>

   <div class="top25landingpage">

	<table id="top25_nav">
		<tr>
			<td rowspan="2">
				<img src="/images/cwe_top_25_logo_white.png" width="200" height="200" alt="CWE Top 25 logo"
				title="CWE Top 25 logo" style="float: left;padding:10px 15px"></td>
			<td colspan="3">
				<p style="font-size:110%;">Welcome to the 2025 Common Weakness Enumeration (CWE&trade;) Top 25 Most Dangerous
					Software Weaknesses list (CWE&trade; Top 25). This list demonstrates the currently most common and impactful
					software weaknesses.</p>
				<p style="font-size:110%;">Often easy to find and exploit, these can lead to exploitable vulnerabilities that
					allow adversaries to completely take over a system, steal data, or prevent applications from working.</p>
			</td>
		</tr>
		<tr class="centerlinks" style="white-space:nowrap;">
			<td><span class="top25_list"><a href="/top25/archive/2025/2025_cwe_top25.html">2025 Top 25 List</a></span></td>
			<td><span class="top25_key_points"><a href="/top25/archive/2025/2025_key_insights.html">Key Insights</a></span></td>
			<td><span class="top25_methodology"><a href="/top25/archive/2025/2025_methodology.html">Methodology</a></span></td>			
		</tr>

	</table>

<a name="additional_information" id="additional_information"></a>
<p>The CWE Top 25 Most Dangerous Software Weaknesses List highlights the most severe and prevalent weaknesses behind the 39,080
	<a target="_blank" href="https://www.cve.org/">Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE&trade;) Records</a>
	in this year’s dataset. Uncovering the root causes of these vulnerabilities serves as a powerful guide for investments,
	policies, and practices to prevent these vulnerabilities from occurring in the first place &mdash; benefiting both industry
	and government stakeholders.</p>

<p>The CWE Top 25 can help inform:</p>
<ul>
	<li><strong>Vulnerability Reduction</strong> &ndash; Insights into the common root causes drive valuable feedback into
	vendors’ SDLC and architectural planning, helping to eliminate entire classes of defect (e.g., memory safety, injection)</li>
	<li><strong>Cost Savings</strong> &ndash; Fewer vulnerabilities in product development mean fewer issues to manage
	post-deployment, ultimately saving money and resources</li>
	<li><strong>Trend Analysis</strong> &ndash; Insight into data trends enables organizations to better focus security efforts</li>
	<li><strong>Exploitability Insights</strong> &ndash; Certain weaknesses such as command injection attract adversarial
	attention, enabling risk prioritization.
	<li><strong>Customer Trust</strong> &ndash; Transparency in how organizations address these weaknesses shows commitment
	to product security</li>
</ul>

<p>The <a href="/top25/archive/2025/2025_cwe_top25.html">2025 CWE Top 25</a> is not only a valuable resource for developers
and security professionals, but it also serves as a strategic guide for organizations aiming to make informed decisions in
software, security, and risk management investments.</p>

<p>Also available now:</p>
<ul>
	<li><strong><a href="/top25/archive/2025/2025_kev_list.html">
    2025 CWE Top 10 KEV Weaknesses</a></strong> &mdash; Ranking actively exploited weaknesses by CISA’s KEV Catalog.</li>	
	<li><strong><a href="/top25/archive/2025/2025_onthecusp_list.html">
    2025 “On the Cusp” Weaknesses List</a></strong> &ndash; 15 additional weaknesses that were “on the cusp” of being
    included in the 2025 CWE Top 25.</li>
</ul>

      <h2 class="header">2025 CWE Top 25 Key Insights</h2>

<div class="tablebutton"><a href="/top25/index.html">Top 25 Home</a></div>
            
<p>The “<a href="/top25/archive/2025/2025_cwe_top25.html">2025 CWE Top 25 Most Dangerous Software Weaknesses</a>”
highlights the most severe and prevalent weaknesses behind the 39,080
<a href="https://www.cve.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE&trade;) Records</a>
in this year’s dataset. Uncovering the technical root causes of these vulnerabilities serves as a powerful guide
for investments, policies, and practices to prevent these vulnerabilities from occurring in the first place.</p>

<p>The CWE Top 25 can help inform:</p>

<ul>
    <li><strong>Vulnerability Reduction:</strong> Drive valuable feedback into the SDLC and architectural planning, helping
    to eliminate entire classes of defects (e.g., memory safety, injection).</li>
    <li><strong>Cost Savings:</strong> Fewer weaknesses introduced during product development means fewer vulnerabilities
    to manage post-deployment, where it is more expensive and resource intensive.</li>
    <li><strong>Trend Analysis:</strong> Insight into vulnerability trends enables organizations to better focus and adapt
    their security strategies. Organizations that look across the ecosystem can leverage these trends to identify systemic
    issues and drive collective improvements in software security practices.</li>
    <li><strong>Customer Trust:</strong> Transparency in how organizations address these weaknesses shows commitment to
    product security and not repeating the same mistakes.</li>
    <li><strong>Consumer Awareness:</strong> Educate software consumers about dangerous weaknesses and common
    vulnerabilities, empowering them to make informed decisions when selecting products.</li>
</ul>

<p>The 2025 CWE Top 25 is a valuable resource for developers and security professionals, as well as a strategic guide for
organizations aiming to make informed decisions in software, security, and risk management investments.</p>

<h3>Analysis</h3>

<p>While
<a href="/data/definitions/79.html">CWE-79: Improper Neutralization of Input During Web Page Generation (‘Cross-Site Scripting’)</a>
retains its spot at the top of the Top 25, there were many changes in rankings from last year’s list.</p>

<p>The biggest movers up the list are:</p>

<ul>
    <li><a href="/data/definitions/862.html">CWE-862: Missing Authorization</a>, up 5 from #9 to #4</li>
    <li><a href="/data/definitions/476.html">CWE-476: Null Pointer Dereference</a>, up 8 from #21 to #13</li>
    <li><a href="/data/definitions/306.html">CWE-306: Missing Authentication</a>, up 4 from #25 to #21</li>
</ul>

<p>New entries in the Top 25 are:</p>

<ul>
    <li><a href="/data/definitions/120.html">CWE-120: Buffer Copy without Checking Size of Input ('Classic Buffer Overflow')</a>,
    at #11 with no previous ranking</li>
    <li><a href="/data/definitions/121.html">CWE-121: Stack-based Buffer Overflow</a>, at #14 with no previous ranking</li>
    <li><a href="/data/definitions/122.html">CWE-122: Heap-based Buffer Overflow</a>, at #16 with no previous ranking</li>
    <li><a href="/data/definitions/284.html">CWE-284: Improper Access Control</a>, at #19 with no previous ranking</li>
    <li><a href="/data/definitions/639.html">CWE-639: Authorization Bypass Through User-Controlled Key</a>, up 6 from #30 to #24</li>
    <li><a href="/data/definitions/770.html">CWE-770: Allocation of Resources Without Limits or Throttling</a>, up 1 from #26 to #25</li>
</ul>

<p>Four CWEs were not ranked in 2024 and earlier because of how previous Top 25 calculations were handled. For more
    detailed information, see the <a href="/top25/archive/2025/2025_methodology.html">Methodology</a> page. In short,
    the 2025 CWE Top 25 used all CWE mappings as provided and reviewed by CNAs, without normalizing them back to
    <a href="/data/definitions/1003.html">View-1003: Weaknesses for Simplified Mapping of Published Vulnerabilities</a>,
    a simplified collection of 130 weaknesses commonly used by the
    <a href="https://nvd.nist.gov/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">U.S. National Vulnerability Database (NVD)</a>
    for their enrichment purposes. As a result, the 2025 CWE Top 25 reflects a more accurate picture of real-world
    CWE mappings and offers greater insight into the CWE root cause mapping practices of the broader vulnerability
    management community.</p>

<p>The biggest downward movers are:</p>

<ul>
<li><a href="/data/definitions/787.html">CWE-787: Out-of-Bounds Write</a>, down 3 from #2 to #5</li>
<li><a href="/data/definitions/20.html">CWE-20: Improper Input Validation</a>, down 6 from #12 to #18</li>
<li><a href="/data/definitions/200.html">CWE-200: Exposure of Sensitive Information to an Unauthorized Actor</a>,
down 3 from #17 to #20</li>
<li><a href="/data/definitions/918.html">CWE-918: SSRF</a>, down 3 from #19 to #22</li>
<li><a href="/data/definitions/77.html">CWE-77: Command Injection</a>, down 10 from #13 to #23</li>
</ul>

<p>Entries that fell off the Top 25 are:</p>

<ul>
    <li><a href="/data/definitions/269.html">CWE-269: Improper Privilege Management</a>, down 14 from #15 to #29</li>
    <li><a href="/data/definitions/190.html">CWE-190: Integer Overflow or Wraparound</a>, down 7 from #23 to #30</li>
    <li><a href="/data/definitions/287.html">CWE-287: Improper Authentication</a>, down 17 from #14 to #31</li>
    <li><a href="/data/definitions/400.html">CWE-400: Uncontrolled Resource Consumption</a>, down 8 from #24 to #32</li>
    <li><a href="/data/definitions/798.html">CWE-798: Use of Hard-coded Credentials</a>, down 13 from #22 to #35</li>
    <li><a href="/data/definitions/119.html">CWE-119: Improper Restriction of Operations within the Bounds of a
    Memory Buffer</a>, down 19 from #20 to #39</li>
</ul>

<p>Most of these downward-moving CWEs (except CWE-190 and CWE-918) may have had sharply reduced mappings because,
    in previous years, mappings to their child CWEs were rolled up to them due to View-1003 normalization. For example,
    CWE-269 had 219 CVEs in non-normalized data this year, while one of its children (CWE-250) was mapped by 88 CVEs.
    If all of CWE-269’s children had been normalized to CWE-269, it would have had 633 CVEs this year. This likely
    would have kept it in the Top 25. Notably, many of these CWEs are more abstract and Discouraged for mapping,
    which may have led CNAs to use more precise mappings.</p>

<h3>Mapping Usage</h3>

<p>Every CWE on cwe.mitre.org is annotated with a “<a href="/documents/cwe_usage/guidance.html">mapping usage recommendation</a>”
that suggests whether the CWE is recommended for vulnerability root cause mapping given its level of abstraction and
actionability. These include Allowed, Allowed-with-Review, Discouraged, and Prohibited.</p>

<p>The CWEs in the 2025 Top 25 received 28,336 total mappings and had the following mapping usage recommendations:</p>

<ul>
    <li>17 Allowed – 22,438 maps (79.19% of all Top 25 mappings)</li>
    <li>5 Allowed-with-Review – 4,363 maps (15.40%)</li>
    <li>3 Discouraged – 1,535 maps (5.42%)</li>
</ul>

<p>In contrast, last year’s 2024 Top 25 had the following mapping usage recommendations:</p>

<ul>
    <li>15 Allowed – 16,298 maps (82.33% of all Top 25 mappings)</li>
    <li>4 Allowed-with-Review – 1,481 maps (7.48%)</li>
    <li>6 Discouraged – 2,017 maps (10.19%)</li>
</ul>

<p>The decline in mappings to “Discouraged” CWEs suggests a marked improvement in root cause mapping practices, since
    other prior years have been in the 10% or 11% range. Even using data normalized to View-1003 as in past years, there
    was still a reduction. This is best illustrated by the removal of two Discouraged CWEs from the Top 25, namely CWE-119
    and CWE-400.</p>

<p>The Top 25 Team encourages
    <a href="https://www.cve.org/ProgramOrganization/CNAs" target="_blank">CVE Numbering Authorities (CNAs)</a>
    to continue striving for actionable and precise CWE mappings in their CVE Records.</p>

<p>For more information about root cause mapping, see our
    <a href="/documents/cwe_usage/guidance.html">CVE-to-CWE Root Cause Mapping Guidance</a>.</p>

<h3>Abstraction</h3>

<p>CWE contains over 900 weaknesses that range from abstract and conceptual to precise and technology- or language-specific.
    A precise weakness will have a parent weakness that is more abstract, which may also have parent weaknesses, and so on.</p>

<p>There are four types of weakness abstractions, from most abstract to most specific:
    <a href="/documents/glossary/index.html#Pillar%20Weakness">Pillar</a>,
    <a href="/documents/glossary/index.html#Class%20Weakness">Class</a>,
    <a href="/documents/glossary/index.html#Base%20Weakness">Base</a>, and
    <a href="/documents/glossary/index.html#Variant%20Weakness">Variant</a>.</p>

<p>For root cause mapping, CWE recommends that Base and Variant level CWEs should be used whenever possible
    to ensure providing adequate specificity and actionability. Class level CWEs may be used  if there is no
    accurate Base or Variant level CWE. Pillar level weaknesses are rarely useful for root cause mapping,
    if ever, with some rare exceptions for unusual or less widely researched weaknesses.</p>

<p>In the 2025 Top 25, the number of mappings based on CWE abstraction were:</p>

<ul>
    <li>Base: 20,351 maps (71.82%) for 15 unique CWEs</li>
    <li>Class: 3,705 maps (13.08%) for 5 unique CWEs</li>
    <li>Compound: 1,750 maps (6.18%) for 1 unique CWE</li>
    <li>Variant: 2,022 maps (7.14%) for 3 unique CWEs</li>
    <li>Pillar: 508 maps (1.79%) for 1 unique CWE</li>
</ul>

<p>The 5 classes in 2025’s Top 25 represent a decrease from 2024’s 9 classes. At least part of this decrease is
    due to the use of raw data, allowing lower-level CWEs to enter the Top 25.</p>

<p>In 2024, the abstraction counts were:</p>

<ul>
    <li>Base: 14,342 maps (72.45%) for 14 unique CWEs</li>
    <li>Class: 3,498 maps (17.67%) for 9 unique CWEs</li>
    <li>Compound: 1,301 maps (6.57%) for 1 unique CWEs</li>
    <li>Variant: 655 maps (3.31%) for 1 unique CWEs</li>
</ul>

<p>It should be noted that the “Compound” CWE is for
    <a href="/data/definitions/352.html">Cross-Site Request Forgery (CSRF)</a>
    (i.e., CWE-352), which is a composite of multiple weaknesses; CWE-352’s abstraction aligns with that of a Base.</p>

<h3>Possible Causes of Rank Shifts</h3>

<p>Given the increase in annual CVE Records, there were unsurprisingly higher CVE counts for most CWEs in this
    year’s dataset analysis. The shift in scoring and the fewer CWE mappings in NVD data in 2024 also contributed
    to this. For example, the number of CVE Records that mapped to CWE-79 increased by over 3,000 this year, and
    CWE-89 by almost 1,000.</p>

<p>As more CNAs contribute their own CWE mappings for CVE Records, it is possible that high-volume CNAs might
    have mapping practices that are different than what has been experienced before. A per-CNA evaluation of mapping
    practices could be informative, but it was outside the scope of this initial analysis.</p>

<p>The Top 25 team is excited to see more and more CNAs routinely providing CWE mappings at the time of disclosure:</p>

<ul>
    <li>In the 2024 Top 25 dataset, 53% of CVE Records had a CWE mapping provided by the publishing CNA.</li>
    <li>In the 2025 Top 25 dataset, this metric increased to 67%, an increase of 14% year over year.</li> 
</ul>

<p>The <a href="https://www.cve.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CVE Program</a>
publishes regular <a target="_blank" href="https://www.cve.org/About/Metrics">metrics</a>,
including the
<a target="_blank" href="https://www.cve.org/About/Metrics#CNAEnrichmentRecognition">CVE Numbering Authority (CNA) Enrichment Recognition List</a>,
which may be of interest to Top 25 readers.</p>

   <div class="top25methodologypage">
	<h2 class="header">2025 CWE Top 25 Methodology</h2>
<div class="tablebutton"><a href="/top25/index.html">Top 25 Home</a></div>


<p>The “<a href="/top25/archive/2025/2025_cwe_top25.html">2025 CWE Top 25 Most Dangerous Software Weaknesses</a>”
list was calculated by analyzing public vulnerability information in
<a target="_blank" href="https://www.cve.org/">Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE&trade;) Records</a>
for CWE
<a href="/documents/cwe_usage/guidance.html">root cause mappings</a>.</p>

<p>This year’s dataset included 39,080 CVE Records for vulnerabilities published between June 1, 2024 and June 1, 2025.
   Data was initially pulled on July 23, 2025, to share with
   <a href="https://www.cve.org/ProgramOrganization/CNAs" target="_blank">CVE Numbering Authority(CNA)</a>
   community partners for review. Data was pulled again on November 17, 2025, to ensure the most current
   information was used to calculate the Top 25.</p>

<h3>Dataset Collection/Scoping</h3>

<p>The initial Top 25 dataset comprised all CVE-2024-XXXX and CVE-2025-XXXX published between June 1, 2024,
   and June 1, 2025. To ensure collecting the complete set of publicly available CWE mappings, the Top 25 team
   gathered those in the CVE List published by CNAs or added by
   <a target="_blank" href="https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/news/unlocking-vulnrichment-enriching-cve-data">CISA Vulnrichment</a>
   after publication, both of which are available on CVE.org. The team also cross-referenced any downstream, third-party mappings
   by NVD analysts and published on nvd.nist.gov. The CVE Records were then analyzed using automated scanning to identify those
   that would benefit from re-mapping analysis. These included CVE Records with CWE mappings that:
	<ol>
	<li>Were too abstract or commonly misused
	<ul>
	<li><i>A common mistake made when mapping a vulnerability to a CWE is choosing an abstract or ‘high-level’ entry
      that is less precise and actionable. CWEs at the
      <a href="/documents/glossary/index.html#Base%20Weakness">Base</a> and
      <a href="/documents/glossary/index.html#Variant%20Weakness">Variant</a>
      level ensure adequate specificity, actionability, and root cause information for a vulnerability.</i></li>
	</ul>
	</li>
	<br/>
	<li>Differed from a mapping found using a MITRE internal keyword matcher tool
	<ul>
	<li><i>Drawing on years of previous Top 25 analysis, the CWE team has identified a list of keywords found in CVE
      descriptions that commonly indicate a specific root cause weakness. Although this keyword list is imperfect,
      the CWE team has found it to be a good starting point to identify mappings that could be incorrect. If the
      keyword matcher found a different mapping than was present, the CVE Record was kept in the dataset for
      remapping analysis.</i></li>
	</ul>
	</li>
	</ol>
</p>

<p>Ultimately, the dataset identified for re-mapping analysis &mdash; the Scoped Dataset &mdash; contained 9,468 CVE Records
   (24% of all CVE Records in the dataset) originally published by 281 different CVE Numbering Authorities CNAs.</p>

<h3>LLM Mapping Suggestions</h3>

<p>The 2025 CWE Top 25 was the first to also employ CWE mapping suggestions from an LLM tool developed by
   Chris Madden (<a target="_blank" href="https://www.yahoo.com">Yahoo!</a>) as part of the
   <a href="/community/working_groups.html#rcm_wg">CWE Root Cause Mapping Working Group</a> (RCM WG).</p>

<p>This year, each CVE in the Scoped Dataset was passed through the bulk assignment LLM tool that provided
   a report with additionally suggested CWE information. This helped remap CVE Records with imprecise
   CWE mappings by providing lower-level suggestions for the publishing CNA to consider. The tool utilized
   a grounded LLM that was given the entire CWE corpus as its dataset and trained on sets of accurate
   CVE-to-CWE mappings. Although the LLM-generated suggested mappings were not always selected, we believe
   they acted as a good starting point for reviewers to perform re-analysis on the CVE Records in certain
   cases. In some cases, the tool appeared to infer potential mappings from references containing low-level
   details that would likely be missed by human analysts due to limitations of time or expertise.</p>

<p>The CWE RCM WG will continue to strengthen this capability and work towards the
   “new and the next” in enabling decentralized, accurate root cause mapping analysis at scale.</p>

<h3>CNA Expert Collaboration</h3>

<p>CNAs are best positioned to provide accurate CWE mapping determinations compared to third-party analysts, as CNAs are the
   authority for vulnerability information within their CNA scope and those closest to the products themselves. To engage
   this expert community in helping create the 2025 Top 25, the Scoped Dataset was divided into batches of CVEs based on
   the CNA who published them, typically with one batch for CVE Records mapped to abstract CWEs, and a separate batch for
   CVE Records with differences based on the internal keyword matcher. On August 19, 2025, the Top 25 team emailed each CNA
   to review their batches.</p>

<p>On November 17, 2025, the team finalized any CWE mapping changes provided by CNAs. Of the 9,468 CVE Records sent for review
   by the 281 different CNAs, the Top 25 team received feedback on 2,459 CVE Records (26% of total requested) from 170 CNAs
   (60% of total contacted) either correcting or confirming existing CWE mappings.</p>

<p>The CWE Team plans to leverage the CNA mapping feedback for potential strengthening of the CWE root cause mapping guidance
   and documentation.</p>

<h3>Dataset Refinement</h3>

<p>For the remaining CVE Records in the Scoped Dataset that did not receive CNA review, the Top 25 team analyzed a prioritized
   subset that included the highest-producing CNA with 1,884 CVE Records in the dataset.</p>

<p>The team discovered that the high-producing CNA frequently mapped to a correct lower-level CWE (e.g., a base or variant)
   but also included the higher-level parent CWE as well. The Top 25 ranking calculations consider every CWE mapping, so the
   inclusion of these parent CWE mappings influenced the dataset considerably. To resolve this, the CWE team removed all of
   this CNA’s CWE mappings where one of its “child” CWEs were also mapped. This significantly reduced the number of more abstract,
   less actionable CWE mappings in the Top 25.</p>

<p>The Top 25 team also reviewed CVE Records published by the MITRE
   <a target="_blank" href="https://www.cve.org/ResourcesSupport/Glossary#glossaryCNALR">CNA of Last Resort</a> (MITRE CNA-LR),
   of which there were 1,266 in the dataset. It is important to note that traditional CNAs and CNAs of Last Resort operate
   very differently. Within the CVE Program, the MITRE CNA-LR (one of three CNA-LRs) is authorized to assign CVE IDs and
   publish corresponding CVE Records within the MITRE Top Level Root’s scope for vulnerabilities not covered by the Scope
   of another CNA. It exists to serve when no appropriate CNA is available or willing &mdash; often cases with limited information.</p>

<p>Like many CNAs, the MITRE CNA-LR is investing in the CVE “Quality Era” and exploring ways to improve data enrichment
   even in scenarios of limited information &mdash; always with the goal of supporting defenders who rely on
   CVE information every day. The Top 25 team reviewed 738 of the total 1,266 MITRE CNA-LR published CVE Records
   in the Scoped Dataset, prioritizing those with adequate mapping information provided by the first party researcher
   that engaged the CNA-LR, and deferring those that would require deeper, more time-intensive analysis.</p>

<h3>Removal of CWE Mapping Normalization</h3>

<p>In prior years, before calculating the Top 25, all CWE mappings were normalized to
   <a href= "/data/definitions/1003.html">View-1003: Weaknesses for Simplified Mapping of Published Vulnerabilities</a>.
   This was because the NVD, as part of its CVE Record enrichment process, typically maps CVE Records to CWEs within
   View-1003, a simplified collection of 130 weaknesses. Records that did not map to a CWE within that View would be marked as
   “NVD-CWE-Other”, or “NVD-CWE-noinfo” in cases where there was not information to make a determination.</p>

<p>This meant that in prior years, any CWE mapping not present in View-1003 was forced to the next closest ancestor
   in that collection, and if no valid ancestor existed, that mapping was excluded entirely.</p>

<p>For the first time in 2025, the Top 25 used the actual CWE mappings as provided, without normalizing them back to
   View-1003. As a result, the 2025 CWE Top 25 reflects a more accurate picture of real-world CWE mappings and offers
   greater insight into the CWE root cause mapping practices of the broader vulnerability management community.</p>

<h3>Scoring</h3>

<p>After the collection, scoping, and remapping process, a scoring formula was used to calculate a rank order of weaknesses
   that combines the frequency (the number of times that a CWE is the root cause of a vulnerability) with the average severity
   of each of those vulnerabilities when they are exploited (as measured by the
   <a target="_blank" href="https://nvd.nist.gov/vuln-metrics/cvss">Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS)</a>
   v3.0 or v3.1 base score). In both cases, the frequency and severity are normalized relative to the minimum and
   maximum values observed in the dataset. These metrics are presented as “<strong>count</strong>” and
   “<strong>average_CVSS</strong>”, respectively in the following formulas. Due to differences in the way CVSS base
   scores are calculated across versions, only CVE Records that contain CVSS version 3.0 or 3.1 data were considered
   in the calculations.</p>

<p class="redbold">Frequency</p>

<p>The scoring formula calculates the number of times a CWE was mapped to a CVE Record within the NVD.</p>

<p class="indent">
	<strong>Freq = {count(CWE_X' &isin; NVD) for each CWE_X' in NVD}</strong>
</p>

<p class="indent">
	<strong>Fr(CWE_X) = (count(CWE_X &isin; NVD) - min(Freq)) / (max(Freq) - min(Freq))</strong>
</p>

<p class="redbold">Severity</p>

<p>The scoring formula calculates the average CVSS score of all CVE Records that map to the CWE. The equation below is
   used to calculate this value.</p>

<p class="indent">
	<strong>Sv(CWE_X) = (average_CVSS(CWE_X) - min(CVSS)) / (max(CVSS) - min(CVSS))</strong>
</p>

<p class="redbold">Danger Score</p>

<p>The level of danger presented by a particular CWE was then determined by multiplying the severity score by the frequency score.</p>

<p class="indent">
	<strong>Score(CWE_X) = Fr(CWE_X) * Sv(CWE_X) * 100</strong>
</p>

<p>With this scoring approach:</p>
	<ul>
      <li>Weaknesses that were rarely discovered will not receive a high Frequency score, regardless of the typical consequence
         associated with any exploitation. If developers are not making a particular mistake, then the weakness should not be
         highlighted in the CWE Top 25.</li>
      <li>Weaknesses whose exploitation was of low impact will not receive a high Severity score, regardless of how common it
         was in the dataset. If the weakness typically results in low-impact exploited vulnerabilities, then the weakness
         should not be highlighted in the CWE Top 25.</li>
      <li>Weaknesses that are both common and caused significant harm will receive the highest scores.</li>
	</ul>
</p>
